01

Light is not an inversion. Accents darken rather than lighten — #7FD4F5 becomes #156587 and #E09A5C becomes #964A14 — because a pale accent on a pale ground has nowhere to go. The four outcome hues darken by the same rule.

02

Prose weight goes from 300 to 400 in light. Thin serif on a bright ground loses its stroke, and this is the only typographic value a theme is allowed to change.

03

Elevation reverses direction: in dark, raised surfaces get lighter; in light, they get whiter than the page while the page itself is tinted. Same token names, opposite ramp order.

04

Four themes, not two: glacial dark, glacial light, ember dark, ember light, each a set of the same fourteen slots. Swapping ruleset changes the family; swapping light and dark changes the mode.

The slot contract — what a user can change

14 colours · 1 type weight · everything else fixed
ground · 5 slots
voidsunkenbaseraisedoverlay
User picks void; the other four are derived along a lightness ramp and can be overridden individually.
ink · 4 slots
primarysecondarymutedhairline
Auto-checked against ground: primary ≥ 7:1, secondary ≥ 4.5:1, muted ≥ 4.5:1. A failing value is corrected and the user is told.
accent · 2 slots
accent · player progresspressure · world clocks
The only hard rule in the whole contract: these two must differ by at least 60° of hue. Player progress and world pressure never share a colour.
outcome · 3 slots
▲ strong hit◐ weak hit▼ miss✦ match — locked
Editable, but every outcome also carries its glyph, so a colourblind or badly-chosen palette still reads. Match stays violet — it is the one signal that must never be mistaken.
how a user edits

Start from one of the four built-in themes, change slots, save under a name. Themes are a small JSON file that can be exported and shared — the same distribution model as the rulesets themselves. A theme that fails a contrast rule still saves; the app corrects at render and shows a note in the theme editor rather than refusing.

what stays out of reach

Fonts, spacing, radius, motion timings, glyphs, and the shapes of tracks and clocks. Those carry the meaning that makes a screen readable at a glance; a colour cannot break them, a font size can. One exception: prose weight, which light mode already moves, and which a user can nudge one step.

Decided — 9a plus 9b as "Paper"

Four built-in themes — glacial and ember, each dark and light — with 9b shipped as a fifth called something like "Paper" for people who want white. Full colour themability on top of that, through the fourteen slots above, with the hue-separation and contrast rules enforced at render rather than at save.

One thing worth deciding now: the sector map's starfield cannot survive a light theme. Either it stays dark in all themes, as a chart on a dark plate, or the starfield is dropped in light and the hex grid carries it alone. I would keep it dark.

Ticks are drawn as the tabletop draws them: four strokes closing a box. Partial boxes are legible from across the room, which a percentage fill never is — and a progress roll counts filled boxes, so the eye must count the same thing the rules do.

02

Two shapes, two hues, two authors: square ice boxes you fill, a round ember dial the world fills. The dual-track challenge puts them either side of a hairline so neither reads as progress toward the other.

03

The pending mark is a dashed ghost box: the suggestion pattern from 1b, unchanged. ⌥M marks, ⌥⇧M reverses, and reversal is an ordinary action, not an undo dialog.

04

Archived tracks stay in the panel at 50%, greyed out of the accent range entirely. Nothing that happened leaves the screen; it just stops being loud.

01

Boxes become lit bars. Individual ticks are quiet — a 140ms fill, no glow change — and the ceremony is reserved for the transition to rollable: an aurora sweep, the border warming to mint, one line of copy telling you the track is ready.

02

The threat clock becomes a pill row rather than a dial, so both halves of a scene challenge read on the same axis. Cheaper to scan mid-roll; weaker at signalling that the two timers have different authors.

03

Half-filled bars are ambiguous: is that box counted or not? A progress roll needs the answer instantly, and the bar makes you read the number instead of the shape.

04

Glow does the work here, which spends the theme's quiet on tracks rather than prose — and it degrades badly under reduced-motion, where the ceremony simply doesn't happen and nothing marks the moment.

Recommendation

2a, with one borrowing from 2b: the "ready to resolve" state. Tally strokes stay countable and keep tracks honest, while the aurora sweep is worth keeping for the one moment per track that deserves ceremony. Everything else marks like an instrument.

02

The offer block is dashed and faintly breathing: nothing has happened yet. Enter burns, Esc takes the miss, ⌥Enter opens a two-field adjust. Declining leaves the block in place, greyed and dated — the audit trail is visible, not archived.

03

04

The verdict card is a discrete object in the log: outcome rule on top, dice in the middle, every available response as a chip. Provenance lives in a footer strip, so "what table, what dice, what source" needs no expansion.

02

Chips are ordered by consequence, and "just write" is always the last, dashed one — the app's stated position that ignoring it is a legitimate move. After a choice the card collapses to a single line and the chosen chip stays visible.

03

Momentum is a bar chart of the legal range, not a number: the lit column is where you are, dark columns are the ceiling. When an impact drops max to 9 the whole chart loses a column — the state change Event 25 says players always miss becomes physically visible.

04

1a for the journal, borrowing 1b's momentum chart and provenance footer.
